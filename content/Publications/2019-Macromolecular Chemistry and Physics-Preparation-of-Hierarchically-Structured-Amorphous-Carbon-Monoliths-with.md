# Preparation of Hierarchically Structured Amorphous Carbon Monoliths with Closed Spherical Mesopores via the Lower Critical Solution Temperature Phase Transition

**Authors:** Jeon, Seung-Yeol; Jeon, Hyeonyeol; Park, Tae Joon; Kang, Min Kwan; Cho, Byoung-Ki; Hwang, Seung Sang; Hur, Kahyun
**Journal:** Macromolecular Chemistry and Physics
**Year:** 2019
**Volume:** 220
**Pages:** 1900165
**Link:** [DOI: 10.1002/macp.201900165](https://doi.org/10.1002/macp.201900165)


## Abstract / Summary
Some polymer mixture systems become immiscible above a specific temperature, the so-called lower critical solution temperature (LCST). In this work, the LCST behavior of a mixture of poly(ethylene oxide)-block-poly(propylene oxide)-block-poly(ethylene oxide) triblock copolymers and phenolic resin oligomers is observed, and the corresponding phase transition is exploited to develop a facile route to hierarchically structured carbon monoliths. Whereas evaporation-induced self-assembly generates hexagonal channels in the monoliths, an additional phase transition at the LCST leads to an ordered arrangement of isolated pores. The fabrication method involves annealing the gel-phased mixture with polymeric microbeads in a 3D-structured mold at the LCST, followed by thermosetting and a carbonization process. The LCST phase transition behavior is observed experimentally by in situ small-angle X-ray scattering, optical transparency measurements, differential scanning calorimetry, and infrared spectroscopy. The fundamental mechanism of the LCST phase transition is further investigated by atomistic molecular dynamics simulations.


## Layman's Summary
**The Concept:**
Imagine trying to build a sponge that is mostly empty space to make it incredibly light, but instead of the holes connecting like a normal sponge, each hole is trapped in its own little bubble. In [[Materials Science|materials science]], researchers are trying to create these kinds of "closed-pore" structures out of carbon to make the ultimate insulators—materials that can block heat, sound, and electricity perfectly. 

**The Problem:**
The main challenge is a frustrating trade-off. To make a material a great insulator, you want it to have as many pores (empty spaces) as possible. However, when you create a lot of pores, they tend to link up and form tunnels. Once these tunnels form, heat and sound can easily travel through them, ruining the insulating effect. It is incredibly difficult to achieve high porosity while keeping the pores isolated from one another.

**The Solution:**
To solve this, the researchers developed a clever baking recipe using special [[Polymers|polymers]]. 
*   **The Mixture:** They mixed a block copolymer (a long-chain molecule) with a carbon-based resin.
*   **The Temperature Trick:** This mixture has a unique property called the Lower Critical Solution Temperature (LCST). When heated to exactly 60 °C, the mixture naturally undergoes a phase transition and separates into distinct, isolated droplets.
*   **The Mold:** They poured this gel-like mixture into a 3D-printed mold along with tiny plastic beads and heated it. 
*   **The Structure:** The heat triggered the LCST separation, and through an evaporation-induced [[Self-assembly|self-assembly]] process, it organized into a highly ordered pattern. 
*   **The Bake:** Finally, they baked the mixture at extreme temperatures. This process turned the resin into solid carbon and completely burned away the plastic beads, leaving behind a solid structure filled with tiny, closed bubbles.

**Why This is Special:**
*   **Trapped Bubbles:** The resulting carbon material is packed with spherical pores that do not touch each other, effectively stopping heat and sound in their tracks.
*   **Hierarchical Structure:** It features an organized structure on multiple levels, from the visible macro-shape down to the [[Nanoscale|nanoscale]] pores.
*   **Complex Shapes:** The method is so versatile that the team successfully cast the carbon into intricate 3D shapes, such as a miniature Korean pagoda, which retained its detailed features even after shrinking during the final high-heat baking process.

**The Takeaway:**
This new method proves that we can overcome the traditional trade-off between high porosity and connected pores. By harnessing the natural separation of polymer mixtures at specific temperatures, we can design super-insulating, incredibly lightweight carbon materials in almost any shape. This could pave the way for next-generation thermal and acoustic insulation for buildings, aerospace components, and advanced electronics.
