# Linking experiment and theory for three-dimensional networked binary metal nanoparticle–triblock terpolymer superstructures

**Authors:** Li, Zihui; Hur, Kahyun; Sai, Hiroaki; Higuchi, Takeshi; Takahara, Atsushi; Jinnai, Hiroshi; Gruner, Sol M.; Wiesner, Ulrich
**Journal:** Nature Communications
**Year:** 2014
**Volume:** 5
**Pages:** 3247
**Link:** [DOI: 10.1038/ncomms4247](https://doi.org/10.1038/ncomms4247)

![[2014NatComm - Linking experiment and theory for three-dimensional networked binary metal nanoparticle–triblock terpolymer superstructures_infographic.jpg]]

## Abstract / Summary
Controlling superstructure of binary nanoparticle mixtures in three dimensions from self-assembly opens enormous opportunities for the design of materials with unique properties. Here we report on how the intimate coupling of synthesis, in-depth electron tomographic characterization and theory enables exquisite control of superstructure in highly ordered porous three-dimensional continuous networks from single and binary mixtures of metal nanoparticles with a triblock terpolymer. Poly(isoprene-block-styrene-block-(N,N-dimethylamino)ethyl methacrylate) is synthesized and used as structure-directing agent for ligand-stabilized platinum and gold nanoparticles. Quantitative analysis provides insights into short- and long-range nanoparticle–nanoparticle correlations, and local and global contributions to structural chirality in the networks. Results provide synthesis criteria for next-generation mesoporous network superstructures from binary nanoparticle mixtures for potential applications in areas including catalysis.


## Layman's Summary
Scientists have developed a highly controlled method to organize tiny metal particles—specifically platinum and gold [[Nanoparticles|nanoparticles]]—into complex, highly porous three-dimensional networks. Achieving this level of structural control at the nanoscale is a major scientific goal, as it paves the way for creating next-generation materials with superior properties for use in energy storage, such as high-charge electrodes, and [[Catalysis|catalysis]].

To create these structures, the researchers used a customized, three-part polymer chain known as a **triblock terpolymer** to act as a structural mold. When mixed with the metal nanoparticles in a solution, this polymer naturally self-assembles into a highly ordered, intertwining 3D lattice called an **"alternating gyroid" network**. 

The secret to controlling exactly where the metal particles end up within this intricate mold lies in **ligands**, which are special molecules attached to the surface of the nanoparticles. By carefully designing the density and chemical properties of these ligand coatings, the scientists could dictate how the nanoparticles interact with the different sections of the polymer. For instance, certain chemical repulsions force gold nanoparticles to group tightly in the structural "nodes" or junctions of the network, while platinum particles arrange themselves more broadly. 

Once the particles successfully self-assembled into the desired arrangement, the researchers used a plasma treatment to safely burn away the organic polymer mold. Because the metal nanoparticles were sufficiently connected to one another, the structure did not collapse, leaving behind a **freestanding, continuous 3D porous metal network**. 

To confirm their results, the team combined advanced 3D electron microscopy (tomography) with specialized computer simulations. The theoretical computer models closely matched the physical experiments, proving that scientists can now effectively predict and program the exact 3D spatial arrangement of mixed metal nanoparticles simply by tweaking their chemical coatings.
