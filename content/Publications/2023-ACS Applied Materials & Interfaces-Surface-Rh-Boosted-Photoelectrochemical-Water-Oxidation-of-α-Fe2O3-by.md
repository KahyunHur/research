# Surface Rh-Boosted Photoelectrochemical Water Oxidation of α-Fe2O3 by Reduced Overpotential in the Rate-Determining Step

**Authors:** Kim, Young-Min; Hong, Yerin; Hur, Kahyun; Kim, Min-Seok; Sung, Yun-Mo
**Journal:** ACS Applied Materials & Interfaces
**Year:** 2023
**Volume:** 15
**Pages:** 37290-37299
**Link:** [DOI: 10.1021/acsami.3c04458](https://doi.org/10.1021/acsami.3c04458)

![[2023ACSAMI - Surface Rh-Boosted Photoelectrochemical Water Oxidation of α-Fe2O3 by Reduced Overpotential in the Rate-Determining Step_infographic.jpg]]

## Abstract / Summary
The photoelectrochemical behavior of Rh cluster-deposited hematite (α-Fe2O3) photoanodes (α-Fe2O3@Rh) was investigated. The interactions between Rh clusters and α-Fe2O3 nanorods were elucidated both experimentally and computationally. A facile UV-assisted solution casting deposition method allowed the deposition of 2 nm Rh clusters on α-Fe2O3. The deposited Rh clusters effectively enhanced the photoelectrochemical performance of the α-Fe2O3 photoanode, and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) and Mott–Schottky analysis were applied to understand the working mechanism for the α-Fe2O3@Rh photoanodes. The results revealed a distinctive carrier transport mechanism for α-Fe2O3@Rh and increased carrier density, while the absorbance spectra remained unchanged. Furthermore, density functional theory (DFT) calculations of the oxygen evolution reaction (OER) mechanism corresponded well with the experimental results, indicating a reduced overpotential of the rate-determining step. In addition, DFT calculation models based on the X-ray diffraction (XRD) measurements and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) results provided precise water-splitting mechanisms for the fabricated α-Fe2O3 and α-Fe2O3@Rh nanorods. Owing to enhanced carrier generation and hole transfer, the optimum α-Fe2O3@Rh3 sample showed 78% increased photocurrent density, reaching 1.12 mA/cm–2 at 1.23 VRHE compared to that of the pristine α-Fe2O3 nanorods electrode.


## Layman's Summary
**The Concept:** 
Imagine using just sunlight and a specially designed type of rust to create clean energy. This process, called photoelectrochemical water splitting, uses light-absorbing materials to act like artificial leaves. They harvest solar energy and use it to break water molecules apart, producing clean hydrogen for fuel and oxygen as a byproduct. 

**The Problem:** 
To make this work, scientists often use hematite (a common form of iron oxide, or rust) to absorb the sunlight. While hematite is incredibly cheap, abundant, and stable, its natural performance is quite sluggish. The energy it absorbs from the sun often gets lost inside the material before it can reach the surface to do the hard work of splitting water. To fix this, scientists add noble metals to speed up the reaction, but these metals are generally too rare and expensive to use in large amounts.

**The Solution:** 
To give the hematite a powerful boost without breaking the bank, the research team modified its surface using a clever, low-cost technique:
*   They grew the hematite into microscopic, rod-like structures.
*   They used a simple UV-light trick to anchor extremely small (2-nanometer) clusters of rhodium—a noble metal—directly onto the hematite rods.
*   By keeping these rhodium clusters strictly at the `[[Nanoscale|nanoscale]]`, they used a tiny amount of the expensive metal while maximizing its surface area to act as a powerful `[[Catalysis|catalyst]]`.

**Why This is Special:** 
*   **Massive Efficiency Boost:** The upgraded rhodium-studded material produced a 78% increase in electrical current from sunlight compared to plain hematite.
*   **Clearing the Bottleneck:** The water-splitting reaction happens in multiple steps. The rhodium clusters specifically lowered the "overpotential"—which is the extra energy hurdle required to force the absolute hardest, slowest step of the reaction to happen.
*   **Better Charge Flow:** The rhodium made it much easier for the electrical charges generated by the sunlight to move quickly through the material and into the water without getting trapped or lost.
*   **Proven by Supercomputers:** The team didn't just guess why it worked; they used advanced computer modeling called `[[DFT]]` (Density Functional Theory) to map out the reaction atom by atom. The computer models perfectly matched their real-world experiments, proving exactly how the rhodium shifts electrons around to stabilize the reaction.

**The Takeaway:** 
This clever leap in `[[Materials Science|materials science]]` proves that strategically placing microscopic clusters of under-explored noble metals like rhodium can drastically improve our ability to harvest solar energy. By clearing the chemical "traffic jams" on the surface of cheap materials, this research brings us a significant step closer to producing affordable, 100% green hydrogen fuel directly from water and sunlight.
