# Intrinsic photonic wave localization in a three-dimensional icosahedral quasicrystal

**Authors:** Jeon, Seung-Yeol; Kwon, Hyungho; Hur, Kahyun
**Journal:** Nature Physics
**Year:** 2017
**Volume:** 13
**Pages:** 363-368
**Link:** [DOI: 10.1038/nphys4002](https://doi.org/10.1038/nphys4002)

![[2017NatPhys - Intrinsic photonic wave localization in a three-dimensional icosahedral quasicrystal_infographic.jpg]]

## Abstract / Summary
Unlike the usual picture of Anderson localization, in three-dimensional quasicrystals light waves can localize without disorder, thanks to their short mean free path.


## Layman's Summary
**The Concept:**
Imagine a maze for light. Usually, light travels in straight lines through clear glass or scatters randomly through a fog. Scientists have been exploring a middle ground: structures called "quasicrystals." These are materials that are perfectly ordered and follow strict geometric rules, yet they never repeat their patterns like a normal crystal does. The vision was to see if this unique "ordered but non-repeating" geometry could force light to stop in its tracks and stay trapped in one place.

**The Problem:**
In standard [[Materials Science]], we know that waves (like light or electrons) can get "stuck" in messy, disordered materials—a phenomenon called [[Anderson Localization]]. However, for decades, researchers debated whether this could happen in a 3D structure that was perfectly clean and had no defects. There was no clear mechanism to explain how a highly ordered quasicrystal could cause waves to halt without the help of random "messiness" or disorder.

**The Solution:**
The research team used advanced computer simulations to study how light moves through a complex, 3-dimensional "icosahedral" quasicrystal. Their approach involved:
*   Building a virtual 3D structure based on a 20-sided shape (a triacontahedron).
*   Testing how light waves transmit through the structure at different angles.
*   Comparing the results to a standard diamond crystal to highlight the differences.
*   Analyzing how long the light "lives" inside the structure before escaping.

**Why This is Special:**
*   **First Discovery:** This is the first time light has been shown to experience "intrinsic" localization in a 3D quasicrystal without needing any extra disorder or randomness.
*   **Short Bounces:** The light waves have a very short "mean free path," meaning they bounce off the internal structure so frequently that they can't easily find a way out.
*   **Directional Trapping:** The localization is "picky"—light gets trapped more strongly when it travels along certain paths that have lower symmetry.
*   **Pseudogaps:** The quasicrystal creates "pseudogaps," which are regions where light is mostly forbidden from traveling, forcing the waves to stay localized instead of passing through ballistically.
*   **Precision Control:** Unlike random materials, the trapping here is a result of the precise [[Nanoscale]] geometry, meaning it can be designed and predicted.

**The Takeaway:**
By proving that light can be trapped by geometry alone, this research opens the door to a new generation of "optical" technology. Instead of using messy materials to control light, we can use precisely engineered quasicrystals. This could lead to hyper-efficient lasers, better sensors, and even light-based [[Neuromorphic Computing]] chips that process information more like a human brain than a traditional computer.
