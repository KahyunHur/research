# Highly tumor-specific DNA nanostructures discovered by in vivo screening of a nucleic acid cage library and their applications in tumor-targeted drug delivery

**Authors:** Kim, Kyoung-Ran; Kang, Seong Jae; Lee, A-Young; Hwang, Dohyeon; Park, Miri; Park, Haedong; Kim, Sanghee; Hur, Kahyun; Chung, Hak Suk; Mao, Chengde; Ahn, Dae-Ro
**Journal:** Biomaterials
**Year:** 2019
**Volume:** 195
**Pages:** 1-12
**Link:** [DOI: 10.1016/j.biomaterials.2018.12.026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.biomaterials.2018.12.026)

![[2019Biomaterials - Highly tumor-specific DNA nanostructures discovered by in vivo screening of a nucleic acid cage library and their applications in tumor-targeted drug delivery_infographic.jpg]]

## Abstract / Summary
Enormous efforts have been made to harness nanoparticles showing extravasation around tumors for tumor-targeted drug carriers. Owing to the complexity of in vivo environments, however, it is very difficult to rationally design a nanoconstruct showing high tumor specificity. Here, we show an approach to develop tumor-specific drug carriers by screening a library of self-assembled nucleic acid cages in vivo. After preparation of a library of 16 nucleic acid cages by combining the sugar backbone and the shape of cages, we screened the biodistribution of the cages intravenously injected into tumor-bearing mice, to discover the cages with high tumor-specificity. This tumor specificity was found to be closely related with serum stability, cancer cell uptake efficiency, and macrophage evasion rate. We further utilized the cages showing high tumor specificity as carriers for the delivery of not only a cytotoxic small molecule drug but also a macromolecular apoptotic protein exclusively into the tumor tissue to induce tumor-specific damage. The results demonstrate that our library-based strategy to discover tumor-targeted carriers can be an efficient way to develop anti-cancer nanomedicines with tumor specificity and enhanced potency.


## Layman's Summary
**The Concept:** 
Scientists want to use tiny particles to carry medicine directly to cancer cells. These microscopic delivery trucks are designed to slip through the leaky blood vessels that naturally surround tumors, delivering treatment exactly where it is needed most.

**The Problem:** 
The human body is incredibly complex, making it extremely difficult to design a delivery truck that *only* goes to the tumor. In most cases, less than 1% of the injected medicine actually reaches the cancer. The vast majority of the particles get trapped and destroyed by the body's natural filter systems—like the liver, kidneys, and spleen—long before they can reach the tumor. 

**The Solution:** 
Instead of trying to guess the perfect design from scratch, the research team created a whole "library" of different options to see what worked best in real life. 
*   They built 16 different wire-like cages out of DNA and RNA (the basic building blocks of genetics).
*   These molecular structures were carefully [[Self-assembly|self-assembled]] into four different geometric shapes using four different chemical backbones.
*   They injected this library into mice with tumors to observe which specific cage designs naturally avoided the body's filters and accumulated in the cancer tissues.

**Why This is Special:** 
*   **Ultimate Precision:** Because these cages are built from genetic material, scientists can program their size and shape down to the sub-nanometer level just by matching base pairs.
*   **Body-Friendly:** These DNA structures act as excellent [[Biomaterials|biomaterials]]; they are safe for the body and do not trigger a harsh immune response.
*   **Survival Skills:** The screening process successfully identified specific cages that were stable in blood, easily swallowed by cancer cells, and great at hiding from the body's cleanup cells (macrophages).
*   **Versatile Delivery:** The winning cages were proven to successfully carry both small chemical drugs and larger, complex proteins ([[Macromolecules|macromolecules]]) directly into the tumor to trigger cancer cell death.

**The Takeaway:** 
This "test them all" strategy takes the guesswork out of designing cancer treatments. By rapidly screening a diverse library of structural cages inside a living body, scientists can quickly discover the most effective vehicles for targeted [[Drug Delivery|drug delivery]]. This paves the way for a highly effective new generation of [[Nanomedicine|nanomedicines]] that can destroy tumors powerfully while leaving healthy tissue completely unharmed.
