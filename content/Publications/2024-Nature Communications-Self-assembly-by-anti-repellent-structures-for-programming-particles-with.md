# Self-assembly by anti-repellent structures for programming particles with momentum

**Authors:** Bae, Junghyun; Yoon, Jinsik; Oh, Sangmin; Kim, Kibeom; Kim, Hyeli; Hur, Kahyun; Cho, Hyesung; Park, Wook
**Journal:** Nature Communications
**Year:** 2024
**Volume:** 15
**Pages:** 10794
**Link:** [DOI: 10.1038/s41467-024-54976-7](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-024-54976-7)

![[2024NatComm - Self-assembly by anti-repellent structures for programming particles with momentum_infographic.jpg]]

## Abstract / Summary
Self-assembled configurations are versatile for applications in which liquid-mediated phenomena are employed to ensure that static or mild physical interactions between assembling blocks take advantage of local energy minima. For granular materials, however, a particle’s momentum in air leads to random collisions and the formation of disordered phases, eventually producing jammed configurations when densely packed. Therefore, unlike fluidic self-assembly, the self-assembly of dry particles typically lacks programmability based on density and ordering symmetry and has thus been limited in applications. Here, we present the self-assembly of particles with momentum, yielding regular arrays with programmable density and symmetry. The key is to embed anti-repellent structures, i.e. traps, that can capture kinetic particles individually and then robustly hold them against collisions with other momentum granules during a dynamic assembly procedure. By using anti-repellent traps, physical interactions between neighbouring particles can be inhibited, resolving many phenomena related to the uncertainty of space-sharing events in granular packing. With our self-assembly strategy, highly dense yet unjammed configurations are demonstrated, which conserve the inherent randomness in the location information of each granule in the trap and are useful for robust multilevel authentication systems as unique applications.


## Layman's Summary
**The Concept:** 
In [[Materials Science|materials science]], [[Self-assembly|self-assembly]] is a process where tiny building blocks automatically arrange themselves into a desired, organized structure. Typically, this is done in a liquid environment because the fluid slows the particles down, allowing them to gently settle into their proper places. The overarching vision of this research is to achieve this same highly ordered self-assembly using dry, fast-moving particles in the air.

**The Problem:** 
When dry particles move through the air, they carry momentum. Without a liquid to slow them down, these particles bounce off surfaces and collide with one another like chaotic, microscopic bumper cars. This uncontrolled bouncing leads to disordered, random clumping, or what scientists call a "jammed" state, making it extremely difficult to program the particles into precise, high-density patterns.

**The Solution:** 
To capture these fast-moving dry particles, the researchers developed specialized "anti-repellent" sticky traps. 
- Using light-based 3D printing techniques, they crafted custom micro-structures made from partially cured [[Polymers|polymer]] adhesives.
- These traps act as [[Smart Materials|smart materials]] that instantly catch colliding particles, securely absorbing their kinetic energy so they don't bounce away.
- The traps are carefully spaced so that each captured particle is held perfectly in place, completely avoiding random collisions with neighboring particles.

**Why This is Special:** 
- It successfully creates highly dense, ordered patterns from fast-moving dry particles without them getting jammed into messy clumps.
- It completely removes the need for liquid environments, unlocking new manufacturing possibilities.
- Even though the particles form a perfectly organized grid on a macro level, the exact microscopic point where each particle makes contact and sticks inside its individual trap is completely random and unique.

**The Takeaway:** 
This breakthrough has a massive impact on physical security and anti-counterfeiting technology. Because the exact sticking point of each particle inside its trap is inherently random, these assembled structures act like un-copiable, physical fingerprints. By scanning these unique microscopic patterns, manufacturers can create ultra-secure, multi-level authentication systems to protect hardware and high-value goods from being forged.
