# Chain Dynamics of Ring and Linear Polyethylene Melts from Molecular Dynamics Simulations

**Authors:** Hur, Kahyun; Jeong, Cheol; Winkler, Roland G.; Lacevic, Naida; Gee, Richard H.; Yoon, Do Y.
**Journal:** Macromolecules
**Year:** 2011
**Volume:** 44
**Pages:** 2311-2315
**Link:** [DOI: 10.1021/ma102659x](https://doi.org/10.1021/ma102659x)

![[2011Macromolecules - Chain Dynamics of Ring and Linear Polyethylene Melts from Molecular Dynamics Simulations_infographic.jpg]]

## Abstract / Summary
The dynamical characteristics of ring and linear polyethylene (PE) molecules in the melt have been studied by employing atomistic molecular dynamics simulations for linear PEs with carbon atom numbers N up to 500 and rings with N up to 1500. The single-chain dynamic structure factors S(q,t) from entangled linear PE melt chains, which show strong deviations from the Rouse predictions, exhibit quantitative agreement with experimental results. Ring PE melt chains also show a transition from the Rouse-type to entangled dynamics, as indicated by the characteristics of S(q,t) and mean-square monomer displacements g1(t). For entangled ring PE melts, we observe g1(t) ∼ t0.35 and the chain-length dependence of diffusion coefficients DN ∝ N−1.9, very similar to entangled linear chains. Moreover, the diffusion coefficients DN remain larger for the entangled rings than the corresponding entangled linear chains, due to about a 3-fold larger chain length for entanglement. Since rings do not reptate, our results point toward other important dynamical modes, based on mutual relaxations of neighboring chains, for entangled polymers in general.

## Layman's Summary
This study uses advanced computer simulations to explore **how different shapes of microscopic plastic molecules (polyethylene) move when they are melted**. Specifically, the researchers compared **"linear" polymers**, which are shaped like typical microscopic strings with two free ends, and **"ring" polymers**, which are closed loops with no ends at all.

When polymer chains become long enough, they bump into each other and get stuck in molecular traffic jams, a process known as **entanglement**. For normal linear polymers, the molecules escape these tangles by slithering through the mess like a snake, a movement heavily dependent on their loose ends leading the way. However, **because ring polymers are closed loops without ends, they cannot slither out of tangles in the same way**.

The simulations revealed several key differences in how these loops behave:

- **Ring polymers take much longer to get tangled:** Because closed loops naturally collapse inward on themselves, a ring polymer has to be about **three times longer than a linear polymer before it starts getting effectively tangled up** with its neighbors.
- **Rings move faster:** Even when they do eventually become entangled, ring polymers are able to travel (diffuse) through the melt noticeably faster than entangled linear polymers.
- **A completely different way of moving:** Since ring polymers cannot slither like normal plastic molecules, the study suggests that they must navigate through the microscopic tangles by **cooperating and shifting together with their neighboring chains**, rather than moving independently.

Ultimately, by understanding how these end-free loops navigate molecular traffic jams, scientists can gain a much deeper understanding of how the physical structure of molecules dictates the way different materials flow, relax, and behave.
