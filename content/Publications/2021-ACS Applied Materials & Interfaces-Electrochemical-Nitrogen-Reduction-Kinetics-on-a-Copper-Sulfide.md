# Electrochemical Nitrogen Reduction Kinetics on a Copper Sulfide Catalyst for NH3 Synthesis at Low Temperature and Atmospheric Pressure

**Authors:** Kong, Jimin; Kim, Min-Seok; Akbar, Ramavi; Park, Hee Young; Jang, Jong Hyun; Kim, Hansung; Hur, Kahyun; Park, Hyun S.
**Journal:** ACS Applied Materials & Interfaces
**Year:** 2021
**Volume:** 13
**Pages:** 24593-24603
**Link:** [DOI: 10.1021/acsami.1c00850](https://doi.org/10.1021/acsami.1c00850)

![[2021ACSAMI - Electrochemical Nitrogen Reduction Kinetics on a Copper Sulfide Catalyst for NH3 Synthesis at Low Temperature and Atmospheric Pressure_infographic.jpg]]

## Abstract / Summary
We studied the electrochemical synthesis of NH3 on Fe–CuS/C catalysts in an alkaline aqueous solution under ambient conditions. The metal chalcogenide catalyst is active in the nitrogen reduction reaction (NRR) for approximately 45 min with an NH3 production yield of 16 μg h–1 cm–2 at −0.4 VRHE, while it decomposes to CuO. The rapid degradation of the catalyst hinders the precise investigation of the NH3 production activity in longer time measurements. Herein, the electrochemical NH3 production rate is enhanced with increased overpotentials when the degradation effect is mitigated in the measurement, which was difficult to observe in the NRR reports. In the Tafel analysis, the exchange current density, heterogeneous rate constant, and transfer coefficient of the Fe–CuS/C catalyst on the NRR were estimated. When the electrode degradation is mitigated, one of the best NH3 production activities among the reported metal sulfide electrochemical NRR catalysts is obtained, which is 42 μg h–1 cm–2 at −0.6 VRHE.


## Layman's Summary
**The Concept:** 
Imagine making the essential ingredient for global agriculture—ammonia fertilizer—using just air, water, and electricity. This vision aims to replace massive, energy-hungry chemical plants with a clean, electrical method that works comfortably at everyday room temperature and normal air pressure.

**The Problem:** 
Currently, the world makes ammonia using a century-old method called the Haber-Bosch process. This process requires extreme heat and intense pressure, meaning it burns massive amounts of fossil fuels and releases billions of tons of carbon dioxide. Scientists want to use a clean electrical method instead, but the chemical bond holding nitrogen gas together is incredibly strong and stubborn. To make matters worse, the special materials—known as `[[Catalysis|catalysts]]`—used to help speed up this electrical reaction tend to break down and degrade very rapidly. Because they fall apart so fast, it is incredibly difficult for researchers to accurately measure how well they are actually working.

**The Solution:** 
The research team tackled this challenge by testing a specially engineered powder made of iron, copper, sulfur, and carbon (an Fe-CuS/C catalyst). Their approach involved:
* Placing the material in a water-based liquid and running an electric current through it to force the nitrogen to react.
* Measuring the ammonia production in short, precise timeframes (like 45-minute windows) before the material had a chance to break down into useless byproducts.
* Using a specialized mathematical model (called Tafel analysis) to accurately calculate the underlying speed, efficiency, and step-by-step mechanics of the chemical reaction.

**Why This is Special:** 
* **Zero Extreme Heat:** The reaction successfully produces ammonia at standard room temperature and atmospheric pressure.
* **Record-Breaking Efficiency:** By finding a clever way to measure the reaction before the material degrades, the team revealed that this catalyst has one of the highest ammonia production rates ever recorded for its specific class of metal sulfides.
* **Eco-Friendly Ingredients:** It uses water as its source of hydrogen rather than fossil fuels, paving the way for a completely zero-carbon process.
* **A Blueprint for the Future:** Applying deep mathematical analysis to this specific electrical reaction provides the broader field of `[[Materials Science|materials science]]` with a much clearer understanding of how to design even better `[[Nanoscale|nanoscale]]` materials in the future.

**The Takeaway:** 
This research proves that with the right mix of metals and clever measuring techniques, we can efficiently pull nitrogen from the air and turn it into ammonia using electricity. It is a critical stepping stone toward a future where our agricultural fertilizers are manufactured sustainably, offering a viable path to eliminate a major source of global greenhouse gas emissions.
