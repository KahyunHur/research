## Abstract / Summary
Block copolymer (BCP) self-assembly (SA) is a useful tool for designing materials with tunable nanostructure as well as controllable multiscale, hierarchical structure. A combination of BCP SA with inorganic materials results in functional hybrid materials with ordered structures down to the nanoscale, thereby exploiting both the advantageous features of structure tunability from BCP SA and functionality from inorganic materials. Rather than a comprehensive review of the entire field of hybrid materials, this overview summarizes a variety of BCP-derived synthetic approaches developed over the last 10–15 years, with emphasis on work by the Wiesner group at Cornell University on hybrid materials with structural characteristics on multiple length scales. This encompasses hybrids with thermodynamic equilibrium-type BCP nanostructures, controlled nonequilibrium-type structure formation processes leading to structural asymmetries, as well as formation of hierarchical BCP materials with control over nanoscale and macroscale structures. Besides the development of wet-chemical methodologies for their synthesis, this overview also features some promising first applications of such materials. Results suggest that BCP SA directed synthetic approaches may provide routes to cost-effective and large-scale materials fabrication potentially useful for both, new materials discovery and study of fundamental structure – property correlations as well as exploration of the materials in a number of today’s most pressing applications including water filtration and energy conversion and storage.


## Layman's Summary
[[Block Copolymer|Block copolymers]]**(BCPs) are special plastics made by linking two or more different types of polymer molecules together into a single chain**. You can think of these different blocks as being like oil and water: they naturally repel each other and want to separate, but because they are chemically chained together, they can't. To compromise, they organize themselves into perfectly regular, microscopic patterns—a process known as [[Self-assembly|self-assembly]]. Depending on the "recipe," these molecules can automatically form tiny flat sheets, microscopic cylinders, or complex 3D sponge-like networks.

While these plastics are amazing at building perfect microscopic templates, they usually lack advanced physical properties—like the ability to conduct electricity, magnetism, or manipulate light. In contrast, inorganic materials like metals, silicon, and ceramics possess these powerful functions but are incredibly difficult and expensive to shape at the nanoscale.

The solution is to combine the best of both worlds. **By using the self-assembling plastics as a microscopic mold or guide, scientists can force functional inorganic materials into perfect, multiscale shapes, creating powerful "hybrid" materials**. Because the materials essentially build themselves from the "bottom-up," this method is a highly scalable and cost-effective way to manufacture advanced technologies.

Scientists achieve this perfect blending through a few clever methods:

- **The Mold Method:** They can let the plastic self-assemble, selectively wash away one of the polymer blocks to create a porous sponge, and then fill those tiny holes with metal or ceramics.
- **The "One-Pot" Method:** They can mix tiny inorganic particles directly into the plastic solution. As the plastic organizes itself, it drags the inorganic particles precisely where they need to go in a single step.
- **Advanced Baking:** Scientists have developed special techniques, such as laser annealing or the "CASH" heating method, to crystalize and harden the inorganic parts without melting or collapsing the delicate microscopic structures.
- **Multiscale Sponges:** Using special kinetic techniques, scientists can create complex materials that have both large "macropores" and incredibly tiny "mesopores" at the exact same time.

Because these hybrid materials combine precise shapes with massive surface areas and functional chemistry, they are poised to revolutionize several major industries:

- **Better Batteries and Fuel Cells:** These perfectly porous structures can be used to create better electrodes. By providing massive surface areas and shorter pathways for energy to flow, they can lead to **cheaper fuel cells and safer, longer-lasting batteries with vastly larger capacities**.
- **Next-Generation Solar Power:** Incorporating these structured networks into solar cells creates highly efficient pathways for electricity to travel and can actually trap light inside the cell, **significantly boosting the efficiency of solar panels**.
- **Advanced Water Filtration:** By taking advantage of the perfectly uniform holes created by block copolymers, scientists can create highly selective **filtration membranes capable of sorting tiny molecules or filtering out specific viruses**.
- **Sci-Fi "Metamaterials":** By arranging metals into incredibly precise 3D networks, scientists can create artificial materials that bend light in ways not found in nature. This could eventually lead to **lenses that completely bypass the physical limits of magnification, or materials capable of perfectly blocking specific heat and sound waves**.
