# Predicting Chiral Nanostructures, Lattices and Superlattices in Complex Multicomponent Nanoparticle Self-Assembly

**Authors:** Hur, Kahyun; Hennig, Richard G.; Escobedo, Fernando A.; Wiesner, Ulrich
**Journal:** Nano Letters
**Year:** 2012
**Volume:** 12
**Pages:** 3218-3223
**Link:** [DOI: 10.1021/nl301209c](https://doi.org/10.1021/nl301209c)

![[2012NanoLett - Predicting Chiral Nanostructures, Lattices and Superlattices in Complex Multicomponent Nanoparticle Self-Assembly_infographic.jpg]]

## Abstract / Summary
“Bottom up” type nanoparticle (NP) self-assembly is expected to provide facile routes to nanostructured materials for various, for example, energy related, applications. Despite progress in simulations and theories, structure prediction of self-assembled materials beyond simple model systems remains challenging. Here we utilize a field theory approach for predicting nanostructure of complex and multicomponent hybrid systems with multiple types of short- and long-range interactions. We propose design criteria for controlling a range of NP based nanomaterial structures. In good agreement with recent experiments, the theory predicts that ABC triblock terpolymer directed assemblies with ligand-stabilized NPs can lead to chiral NP network structures. Furthermore, we predict that long-range Coulomb interactions between NPs leading to simple NP lattices, when applied to NP/block copolymer (BCP) assemblies, induce NP superlattice formation within the phase separated BCP nanostructure, a strategy not yet realized experimentally. We expect such superlattices to be of increasing interest to communities involved in research on, for example, energy generation and storage, metamaterials, as well as microelectronics and information storage.

## Layman's Summary
**[[Self-assembly|Self-assembly]]** is a process where tiny building blocks, like nanoparticles and polymers, automatically arrange themselves into complex, useful structures. However, **predicting the exact final shapes of these mixed materials is incredibly challenging** because there are many different forces at play, such as physical crowding and long-range electrical charges. Because of this complexity, finding the right material design usually requires a lot of experimental trial and error.

To solve this, researchers developed a **new computer modeling method** known as a field theory approach. This tool combines the strengths of different physics theories to **quickly and accurately predict how complex mixtures of nanoparticles and polymers will organize themselves**, providing near-molecular level detail without the need for painfully slow and expensive computer simulations.

Using this new predictive tool, the researchers made two major discoveries about how these materials can assemble:

- **Twisted 3D Networks (Chiral Structures):** The researchers simulated mixing nanoparticles—coated in protective molecules—with a specific type of three-part polymer. They predicted that this mixture would automatically assemble into an **"alternating Gyroid"**. This is a complex, twisted, maze-like structure that lacks mirror symmetry, making it "chiral". This structure forms because the polymers pack around the nanoparticles in a way that herds the particles neatly into the center of the polymer networks.
- **Highly Ordered Grids (Superlattices):** The team also investigated what happens when the nanoparticles carry a strong electrical charge. When these charged particles are mixed into polymers that naturally form flat, alternating layers, the electrical repulsion between the particles forces them to push away from one another. This causes the nanoparticles to arrange themselves into a highly regular, crystal-like grid—**a "[[Superlattice|superlattice]]"—trapped directly inside the polymer layers**. This represents a novel structural strategy that experimental scientists have not yet been able to achieve in a lab.

Ultimately, this new theoretical tool allows scientists to **design advanced hybrid materials much more efficiently**, rather than relying strictly on guessing and testing. These highly organized nanostructures hold great promise for **future technologies in energy generation, better batteries, microelectronics, and advanced [[Metamaterials|metamaterials]]**.
