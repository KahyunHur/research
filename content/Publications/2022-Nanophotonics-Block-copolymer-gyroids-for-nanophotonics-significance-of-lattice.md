# Block copolymer gyroids for nanophotonics: significance of lattice transformations

## Abstract / Summary
A gyroid crystal possesses a peculiar structural feature that can be conceptualized as a triply periodic surface with a constant mean curvature of zero. The exotic optical properties such as the photonic bandgap and optical chirality can emerge from this three-dimensional (3D) morphological feature. As such, gyroid crystals have been considered as the promising structures for photonic crystals and optical metamaterials. To date, several methods have been proposed to materialize gyroid crystals, including 3D printing, layer-by-layer stacking, two-photon lithography, interference lithography, and self-assembly. Furthermore, the discovery of Weyl points in gyroid crystals has further stimulated these advancements. Among such methods, the self-assembly of block copolymers (BCPs) is unique, because this soft approach can provide an easy-to-craft gyroid, especially at the nanoscale. The unit-cell scale of a gyroid ranging within 30–300 nm can be effectively addressed by BCP self-assembly, whereas other methods would be challenging to achieve this size range. Therefore, a BCP gyroid has provided a material platform for metamaterials and photonic crystals functioning at optical frequencies. Currently, BCP gyroid nanophotonics is ready to take the next step toward topological photonics beyond the conventional photonic crystals and metamaterials. In particular, the intrinsic lattice transformations occurring during the self-assembly of BCP into a gyroid crystal could promise a compelling advantage for advancing Weyl photonics in the optical regime. Lattice transformations are routinely considered as limitations, but in this review, we argue that it is time to widen the scope of the lattice transformations for the future generation of nanophotonics. Thus, our review provides a comprehensive understanding of the gyroid crystal and its lattice transformations, the relevant optical properties, and the recent progress in BCP gyroid self-assembly.


## Layman's Summary
**The Concept:** 
Imagine a complex, repeating 3D maze that curves perfectly without any sharp corners—a shape known as a "gyroid." Because of its unique twisting structure, a gyroid can interact with light in mind-bending ways, acting as a special filter or reflector. Scientists want to use these structures to build next-generation optical devices and metamaterials that can trap, bend, or guide light.

**The Problem:** 
The "magic" of gyroids only works for certain types of visible light if the maze is incredibly tiny—specifically at the [[Nanoscale|nanoscale]] (roughly 30 to 300 nanometers wide). Traditional manufacturing methods, like 3D printing or high-tech etching, struggle to build such intricately curved 3D structures at this microscopic size. While macroscopic (large) gyroids are easy to print, the tiny ones needed for advanced light manipulation are historically a huge challenge to make reliably.

**The Solution:** 
Instead of trying to carve or print these tiny shapes, scientists use a chemical trick called [[Self-assembly|self-assembly]]. By using special chained molecules known as [[Block Copolymer|block copolymers]], they can encourage the materials to naturally organize themselves into a perfect gyroid shape. 
* Scientists use solvents and heat to let the polymer chains "wiggle" and flow.
* The chains naturally arrange and lock into the complex 3D gyroid maze.
* This bottom-up approach easily builds structures exactly in the tricky 30–300 nm size range, bridging the gap left by traditional tools.

**Why This is Special:** 
* **Self-Building:** The material practically builds itself into a highly complex 3D shape, bypassing the need for impossibly tiny 3D printers.
* **Exotic Light Control:** The resulting structures can trap light and feature rare optical phenomena like [[Weyl Points|Weyl points]], which act as special, highly efficient pathways for light particles.
* **Turning a Bug into a Feature:** During the chemical assembly process, the gyroid shape slightly stretches and warps (a process called "lattice transformation"). While scientists usually view this stretching as a frustrating defect, this paper argues that we can actually *use* this natural distortion to fine-tune and control light in completely new ways!

**The Takeaway:** 
By mastering how these polymers naturally assemble and stretch, scientists are unlocking a new toolkit for "nanophotonics." Instead of fighting the natural warping of the material during manufacturing, researchers can exploit it to design revolutionary optical chips, advanced sensors, and next-generation light-based computers.
