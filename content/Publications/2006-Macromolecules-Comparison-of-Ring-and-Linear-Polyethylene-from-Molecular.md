# Comparison of Ring and Linear Polyethylene from Molecular Dynamics Simulations

**Authors:** Hur, Kahyun; Winkler, Roland G.; Yoon, Do Y.
**Journal:** Macromolecules
**Year:** 2006
**Volume:** 39
**Pages:** 3975-3977
**Link:** [DOI: 10.1021/ma060274s](https://doi.org/10.1021/ma060274s)

![[2006Macromolecules - Comparison of Ring and Linear Polyethylene from Molecular Dynamics Simulations_infographic.jpg]]

## Layman's Summary
Imagine trying to understand how a pot of cooked spaghetti moves compared to a pot full of closed circular loops. This is similar to the challenge faced by scientists studying polymers, which are long chain-like molecules.

**The Background** Scientists already have a good understanding of how standard, **linear polymers** (like normal spaghetti strands) behave when melted; they slide and slither past one another in a snake-like motion, largely guided by their ends. However, **ring polymers**—which are formed into closed loops with no ends—behave entirely differently. Studying these rings in a real-world laboratory is extremely difficult because it is hard to synthesize pure ring polymers without them accidentally tying into knots or linking together like a chain-link fence.

**The Study's Approach** To get around this physical limitation, the researchers used highly detailed **computer simulations** to model melted polyethylene (a very common plastic) at an atomic level. They simulated both linear chains and circular rings ranging in size from 40 to 300 carbon atoms. Unlike older computer models, this study closely mimicked real-world physical conditions by adjusting for constant temperature and pressure, allowing the simulated plastic to naturally expand or contract.

**Key Findings**

- **Shape and Folding:** The study found that **ring polymers are significantly more compact than their linear counterparts**. When packed tightly together in a melted state, the rings naturally collapse and fold inward, forming tight, compact loops.
- **Density differences:** As linear chains get longer, they pack together more densely in the melt. In contrast, the density of ring polymers remains almost exactly the same regardless of how large the individual rings get.
- **Movement and Tangling (Entanglement):** As linear polymers grow longer than about 110 atoms, they start to heavily intertwine and tangle with one another, which drastically slows down their ability to move. Surprisingly, **the ring polymers did not show this severe tangling effect**, even up to the largest sizes tested in the simulation.
- **Speed:** Even though they don't get tangled, the rings still move slowly. Shorter linear strings actually move faster than ring polymers of the same size. Because the rings are folded into dense, compact loops, all the atoms in the ring are forced to move together cooperatively, which creates drag and slows down the molecule as a whole.

**Why It Matters** By acting as a virtual microscope, this research provides a much clearer picture of how a polymer's fundamental architecture—having free ends versus being a closed loop—completely changes its microscopic structure and movement. The researchers hope these insights will help guide future physical experiments and improve our broader understanding of complex liquid materials.
