# Asymmetric Porous Catalyst Structures for Low-Temperature Photocatalytic Dry Reforming of Methane

**Authors:** Moore, William; Shoji, Shusaku; Tsaur, Lieihn; Yu, Fei; Thedford, R. Paxton; Tait, William R.; Riasi, M. Sadegh; Saha, Aniruddha; Hur, Kayhun; Reese, Austin; Kozbek, Ali Y.; Hesse, Sarah A.; Gruner, Sol M.; Yeghiazarian, Lilit; Sobhani, Sadaf; Suntivich, Jin; Wiesner, Ulrich B.
**Journal:** ACS Nano
**Year:** 2025
**Volume:** 19
**Pages:** 23817-23828
**Link:** [DOI: 10.1021/acsnano.5c04286](https://doi.org/10.1021/acsnano.5c04286)

![[2025ACSNano - Asymmetric Porous Catalyst Structures for Low-Temperature Photocatalytic Dry Reforming of Methane_infographic.jpg]]

## Abstract / Summary
Recent advances in the photocatalytic activation of dry reforming of methane (DRM: CO2 + CH4 → 2CO + 2H2) at low temperature and ambient pressure have generated considerable interest as a promising route to convert greenhouse gases into valuable synthetic gas (syngas). While detailed studies have revealed the mechanisms involved in photocatalytic DRM at metal–semiconductor interfaces, less attention has been devoted to how high-surface-area semiconductor supports may enhance such conversions. Here, we structure triblock terpolymer self-assembly directed sol–gel-derived transition metal oxide (Ta2O5 or TiO2) supports of Rh-loaded photocatalysts into various equilibrium and nonequilibrium derived porous morphologies and show how they modulate single-pass conversion, total production rate, and material efficiency. Supported by in-depth materials characterization, flow, and optics simulations rationalizing observed trends, results reveal record catalyst performance. Our work suggests that asymmetric pore structures simultaneously optimizing mass transport and surface area may be well-suited to maximize photocatalyst performance.


## Layman's Summary
**The Concept:**
Converting harmful greenhouse gases (like carbon dioxide and methane) into useful chemical building blocks usually requires massive amounts of heat. However, scientists are exploring a new method called "photocatalysis," which uses the power of light—instead of extreme heat—to drive these chemical reactions at much lower temperatures.

**The Problem:**
The traditional process for recycling these gases, known as "dry reforming of methane," needs temperatures above 800 °C to work. Pumping that much heat into the system is expensive, and it quickly damages or deactivates the [[Catalysis|catalysts]] (the materials that speed up the reaction). While using light helps lower the temperature, existing light-driven catalysts are usually solid blocks. This means they don't have enough exposed surface area to interact with a large volume of gas quickly, making the gas conversion process too slow for real-world industrial use.

**The Solution:**
To fix this, the researchers created a highly porous, sponge-like structure for their catalyst materials. Here is their approach:
* They mixed light-absorbing metal oxides with special [[Polymers|polymers]].
* Using a process called [[Self-Assembly|self-assembly]], the mixture naturally organized itself into complex microscopic shapes as it dried.
* They baked the material to burn away the polymer template, leaving behind a custom-designed, hollow oxide structure loaded with tiny metal [[Nanomaterials|nanoparticles]].
* They specifically designed an "asymmetric" structure, which features massive, finger-like tunnels reaching deep into the material, with walls covered in even smaller microscopic pores.

**Why This is Special:**
* **Record-Breaking Efficiency:** By combining high surface areas with wide tunnels, they achieved the highest recorded performance for this specific light-driven reaction.
* **Easy Gas Flow:** The large, asymmetric tunnels act like highways, allowing greenhouse gases to easily flow in and out without getting trapped.
* **Maximum Light Exposure:** The tiny pores on the tunnel walls ensure that a huge amount of the catalyst surface is simultaneously exposed to light and gas.
* **Longer Lifespan:** Because the system is driven by light rather than intense heat, the materials avoid the thermal damage and soot buildup that usually ruin traditional catalysts.

**The Takeaway:**
By engineering the physical microscopic "architecture" of a material rather than just changing its chemistry, we can dramatically boost its ability to recycle greenhouse gases. This structural breakthrough brings us one step closer to practically and profitably turning air pollution into valuable synthetic fuels using just the power of light.
