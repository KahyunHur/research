# Direct Access to Hierarchically Porous Inorganic Oxide Materials with Three-Dimensionally Interconnected Networks

**Authors:** Hwang, Jongkook; Jo, Changshin; Hur, Kahyun; Lim, Jun; Kim, Seongseop; Lee, Jinwoo
**Journal:** Journal of the American Chemical Society
**Year:** 2014
**Volume:** 136
**Pages:** 16066-16072
**Link:** [DOI: 10.1021/ja5091172](https://doi.org/10.1021/ja5091172)

![[2014JACS - Direct Access to Hierarchically Porous Inorganic Oxide Materials with Three-Dimensionally Interconnected Networks_infographic.jpg]]

## Abstract / Summary
Hierarchically porous oxide materials have immense potential for applications in catalysis, separation, and energy devices, but the synthesis of these materials is hampered by the need to use multiple templates and the associated complicated steps and uncontrollable mixing behavior. Here we report a simple one-pot strategy for the synthesis of inorganic oxide materials with multiscale porosity. The inorganic precursor and block copolymer are coassembled into an ordered mesostructure (microphase separation), while the in situ-polymerized organic precursor forms organic-rich macrodomains (macrophase separation) around which the mesostructure grows. Calcination generates hierarchical meso/macroporous SiO2 and TiO2 with three-dimensionally interconnected pore networks. The continuous 3D macrostructures were clearly visualized by nanoscale X-ray computed tomography. The resulting TiO2 was used as the anode in a lithium ion battery and showed excellent rate capability compared with mesoporous TiO2. This work is of particular importance because it (i) expands the base of BCP self-assembly from mesostructures to complex porous structures, (ii) shows that the interplay of micro- and macrophase separation can be fully exploited for the design of hierarchically porous inorganic materials, and therefore (iii) provides strategies for researchers in materials science and polymer science.

## Layman's Summary
**The Concept of "Multi-Scale Sponges"** Imagine a sponge that has both tiny, microscopic holes and large, interconnected tunnels. In materials science, materials with this type of structure are called **hierarchically porous materials**. They are highly sought after for use in batteries, chemical separations, and catalysis because they offer the best of both worlds: the large tunnels allow liquids and gases to flow quickly (mass transport), while the tiny holes provide a massive surface area for chemical reactions.

**The Problem with Current Methods** Normally, creating these "multi-scale" porous materials is a laborious and complicated chore. Scientists usually have to use multiple different templates to shape the different sizes of pores, but these templates often clash, mix uncontrollably, and fail to work cooperatively. This often results in poorly defined structures.

**The Breakthrough: A "One-Pot" Solution** The researchers developed a much simpler, **"one-pot" synthesis strategy** called **CHAMPS** (Combined Hierarchical Assembly of Micro- and Macrophase Separation). This method allows them to create inorganic materials, such as silica or titanium dioxide, with a continuous, 3D network of both large and small pores all in a single mixture.

**How It Works** In this single container, the scientists mix an inorganic building block, a special structuring polymer (block copolymer), and an organic resin known as "resol". As the mixture is slightly heated and allowed to cure, two things happen simultaneously:

- **Micro-level structures form:** The inorganic material and the polymer naturally organize themselves into a highly ordered pattern of tiny pores (mesopores).
- **Macro-level structures form:** At the exact same time, the organic resin clumps together to form much larger chunks (macropores) that the tiny structures grow around.

Once this hybrid structure is set, the scientists bake it at a very high temperature (a process called calcination). This intense heat burns away the plastics and resins, leaving behind a perfectly formed, solid inorganic framework filled with a **three-dimensionally interconnected network of microscopic holes and larger tunnels**.

**Real-World Application: Better Batteries** To prove how useful this is, the team used the CHAMPS method to create a titanium dioxide ($TiO_2$) structure and tested it as an anode in a lithium-ion battery. They found that because the large, connected tunnels acted as "reservoirs" that allowed the battery's electrolyte fluid to move easily and wet the material, this new structure **charged and discharged much faster and held its capacity far better** than materials that only had tiny pores.
