# A 3D Optical Metamaterial Made by Self-Assembly

**Authors:** Vignolini, Silvia; Yufa, Nataliya A.; Cunha, Pedro S.; Guldin, Stefan; Rushkin, Ilia; Stefik, Morgan; Hur, Kahyun; Wiesner, Ulrich; Baumberg, Jeremy J.; Steiner, Ullrich
**Journal:** Advanced Materials
**Year:** 2012
**Volume:** 24
**Pages:** OP23-OP27
**Link:** [DOI: 10.1002/adma.201103610](https://doi.org/10.1002/adma.201103610)

![[2012AdvMat - A 3D Optical Metamaterial Made by Self-Assembly_infographic.jpg]]

## Abstract / Summary
Optical metamaterials have unusual optical characteristics that arise from their periodic nanostructure. Their manufacture requires the assembly of 3D architectures with structure control on the 10-nm length scale. Such a 3D optical metamaterial, based on the replication of a self-assembled block copolymer into gold, is demonstrated. The resulting gold replica has a feature size that is two orders of magnitude smaller than the wavelength of visible light. Its optical signature reveals an archetypal Pendry wire metamaterial with linear and circular dichroism.

## Layman's Summary
[[Metamaterials|Metamaterials]] are artificial structures engineered to interact with light in ways that do not happen in nature. While scientists have successfully created these materials before, doing so for visible light is incredibly difficult because the structures need to be unimaginably tiny—just a few tens of nanometers wide—which makes manufacturing them over large areas nearly impossible using traditional methods like laser writing.

To solve this problem, researchers have successfully created the **first 3D optical metamaterial using a process called** "[[Self-assembly|self-assembly]]".

**How They Built It** Instead of trying to manually carve out tiny structures, the scientists used special polymer chains called **block copolymers**, which naturally organize themselves into complex, 3D shapes.

1. They started with a polymer that naturally self-assembles into a "gyroid"—a complex, microscopic 3D maze that has a natural twist or "handedness" to it.
2. They used UV light and chemicals to dissolve one specific part of this plastic maze, creating an empty network of pores.
3. They filled this empty microscopic space with solid gold using a process called electrodeposition.
4. Finally, they removed the remaining polymer, leaving behind a **continuous, 3D sponge-like network of pure gold** surrounded by air. The repeating pattern of this gold structure is only about 50 nanometers wide, which is much smaller than a single wave of visible light.

**Why This Material is Special** Because of its unique, tiny structure, this gold network acts entirely differently than a regular block of gold. It effectively behaves like an entirely "new metal".

- **Incredible Transparency:** Normally, solid gold blocks almost all light. However, because this structure is mostly air and forces the gold into thin, connected wires, it dramatically changes how the electrons in the metal behave. As a result, it becomes highly transparent, especially to red and infrared light.
- **Shape-Shifting Colors:** The material is anisotropic, meaning its structure looks different from different angles. Because of this, the color and the amount of light it reflects change drastically depending on the orientation and polarization of the light shining on it.
- **Light Twisting (Optical Chirality):** Because the 3D gold maze has a natural spiral-like shape, it reacts differently depending on whether the light hitting it is spinning in a "left-handed" or "right-handed" direction.

**The Big Picture** This breakthrough proves that complex, nanoscale optical metamaterials do not have to be painstakingly carved by hand or machine. By allowing special polymers to self-assemble, scientists now have a practical pathway for the **mass production of advanced optical metamaterials**, which could lead to revolutionary new technologies in how we manipulate light.
