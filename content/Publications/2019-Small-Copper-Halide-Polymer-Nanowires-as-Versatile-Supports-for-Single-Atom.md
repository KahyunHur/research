# Copper-Halide Polymer Nanowires as Versatile Supports for Single-Atom Catalysts

**Authors:** Kim, Min-Seok; Park, Haedong; Won, Sung Ok; Sharma, Aditya; Kong, Jimin; Park, Hyun S.; Sung, Yun-Mo; Park, Tae Joon; Moon, Myoung-Woon; Hur, Kahyun

**Pages:** 1903197
**Link:** [DOI: 10.1002/smll.201903197](https://doi.org/10.1002/smll.201903197)

## Abstract / Summary
Single-atom catalysts are heterogeneous catalysts with atomistically dispersed atoms acting as a catalytically active center, and have recently attracted much attention owing to the minimal use of noble metals. However, a scalable and inexpensive support that can stably anchor isolated atoms remains a challenge due to high surface energy. Here, copper-halide polymer nanowires with sub-nanometer pores are proposed as a versatile support for single-atom catalysts. The synthesis of the nanowires is straightforward and completed in a few minutes. Well-defined sub-nanometer pores and a large free volume of the nanowires are advantageous over any other support material. The nanowires can anchor various atomistically dispersed metal atoms into the sub-nanometer pores up to ≈3 at% via a simple solution process, and this value is at least twice as big as previously reported data. The hydrogen evolution reaction activity of −18.0 A mgPt−1 at −0.2 V overpotential shows its potential for single-atom catalysts support.


## Layman's Summary
**The Concept:**
Imagine a microscopic "fishing net" designed to catch and hold individual atoms. This research introduces a new type of nanowire—a long, thin structure made of copper and plastic-like materials—that acts as a perfect support for "single-atom catalysts." These are chemical tools where every single metal atom is put to work, making chemical reactions faster and much more efficient.

**The Problem:**
Many important technologies, like fuel cells, rely on [[Catalysis]] using expensive precious metals like platinum. To save money and resources, scientists want to use individual atoms instead of large chunks of metal. However, these "single atoms" are extremely unstable; they naturally want to clump together like magnets, which ruins their effectiveness. Creating a material that can hold these atoms apart in a stable, cheap, and easy-to-scale way has been a major scientific hurdle.

**The Solution:**
The researchers discovered a way to create **copper-halide polymer nanowires**. Their approach is remarkably simple:
*   They mix common copper salts with sulfur-containing compounds in a liquid.
*   Instead of using high heat or complex machinery, they use sound waves to trigger [[Self-Assembly|self-assembly]].
*   In just a few minutes at room temperature, the chemicals "grow" into a forest of tiny wires filled with microscopic holes (pores) that are perfectly sized to trap and protect metal atoms.

**Why This is Special:**
*   **Simple and Cheap:** It uses inexpensive chemicals and a process that works at room temperature in minutes.
*   **Tiny Traps:** The wires contain "sub-nanometer" pores—holes so small they can cradle a single atom and prevent it from clumping with others.
*   **High Capacity:** These [[Nanomaterials]] can hold twice as many active metal atoms as previous support materials, making them much more powerful.
*   **Highly Versatile:** The same nanowire "net" can be used to hold different metals like gold, silver, or platinum, depending on what chemical reaction is needed.

**The Takeaway:**
This discovery is a major step toward making clean energy and high-tech manufacturing more affordable. By using these [[Polymers|polymer]]-based nanowires, we can build highly efficient systems for producing clean hydrogen fuel or filtering pollutants while using only a tiny fraction of the expensive precious metals we rely on today.
