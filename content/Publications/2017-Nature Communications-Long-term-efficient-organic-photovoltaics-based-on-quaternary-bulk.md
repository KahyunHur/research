# Long-term efficient organic photovoltaics based on quaternary bulk heterojunctions

**Authors:** Nam, Minwoo; Cha, Minjeong; Lee, Hyun Hwi; Hur, Kahyun; Lee, Kyu-Tae; Yoo, Jaehong; Han, Il Ki; Kwon, S. Joon; Ko, Doo-Hyun
**Journal:** Nature Communications
**Year:** 2017
**Volume:** 8
**Pages:** 14068
**Link:** [DOI: 10.1038/ncomms14068](https://doi.org/10.1038/ncomms14068)

![[2017NatComm - Long-term efficient organic photovoltaics based on quaternary bulk heterojunctions_infographic.jpg]]

## Abstract / Summary
A major impediment to the commercialization of organic photovoltaics (OPVs) is attaining long-term morphological stability of the bulk heterojunction (BHJ) layer. To secure the stability while pursuing optimized performance, multi-component BHJ-based OPVs have been strategically explored. Here we demonstrate the use of quaternary BHJs (q-BHJs) composed of two conjugated polymer donors and two fullerene acceptors as a novel platform to produce high-efficiency and long-term durable OPVs. A q-BHJ OPV (q-OPV) with an experimentally optimized composition exhibits an enhanced efficiency and extended operational lifetime than does the binary reference OPV. The q-OPV would retain more than 72% of its initial efficiency (for example, 8.42–6.06%) after a 1-year operation at an elevated temperature of 65 °C. This is superior to those of the state-of-the-art BHJ-based OPVs. We attribute the enhanced stability to the significant suppression of domain growth and phase separation between the components via kinetic trapping effect.


## Layman's Summary
**The Concept:**
Imagine solar cells that aren't heavy, rigid silicon slabs, but are instead made of flexible, "plastic-like" materials. These are called organic photovoltaics. Because they are thin and flexible, they could be printed onto rolls of plastic or used to create "smart windows" that generate power while letting light through.

**The Problem:**
The biggest "kink" in this technology is its lifespan. Inside these cells, different materials are mixed together at a microscopic level. For the cell to work, these materials need to stay perfectly blended. However, these [[Sustainable Polymers|polymers]] and carbon molecules are naturally restless. Especially in the heat of a summer rooftop, they tend to clump together—a process called [[Microphase Separation|phase separation]]. Once they clump, the "highway" that electricity travels on is broken, and the solar cell stops working.

**The Solution:**
Researchers found that "crowding" the mixture is the secret to stability. Instead of the usual two-component mix, they created a "quaternary" (four-part) blend:
*   They mixed two different types of light-harvesting [[Sustainable Polymers|polymers]].
*   They added two different types of "acceptor" molecules.
*   By packing these four different materials together, they created a "traffic jam" (kinetic trapping) that prevents any single material from moving around and clumping.

**Why This is Special:**
*   **Built to Last:** While standard organic cells fail quickly in the heat, this new version kept over 72% of its efficiency even after a full year of baking at 65°C (149°F).
*   **Broadband Absorption:** By using multiple materials, the cell can "eat" more of the sun’s energy, capturing everything from ultraviolet to near-infrared light.
*   **Color Control:** Because the recipe can be adjusted, the solar cells can be tuned to different colors, making them beautiful additions to buildings rather than just industrial tools.
*   **Tiny Architecture:** The study uses advanced [[Nanomaterials]] to ensure that even though the mix is complex, the electricity can still find its way out efficiently.

**The Takeaway:**
This research moves "plastic" solar cells out of the lab and closer to our homes. By using a clever four-part mixture to stop the natural [[Self-assembly|self-assembly]] of clumps, we can finally make solar power that is flexible, colorful, and—most importantly—durable enough for the real world.
