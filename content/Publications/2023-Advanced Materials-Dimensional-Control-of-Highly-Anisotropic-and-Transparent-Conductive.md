# Dimensional Control of Highly Anisotropic and Transparent Conductive Coordination Polymers for Solution-Processable Large-Scale 2D Sheets

**Authors:** Suh, Bong Lim; Kang, Goun; Yoon, Sun Mi; Cho, Sanghyun; Moon, Myoung-Woon; Sung, Yun-Mo; Kim, Min-Seok; Hur, Kahyun
**Journal:** Advanced Materials
**Year:** 2023
**Volume:** 35
**Pages:** 2206980
**Link:** [DOI: 10.1002/adma.202206980](https://doi.org/10.1002/adma.202206980)

![[2023AdvMat - Dimensional Control of Highly Anisotropic and Transparent Conductive Coordination Polymers for Solution-Processable Large-Scale 2D Sheets_infographic.jpg]]

## Abstract / Summary
Controlling the dimensional aspect of conductive coordination polymers is currently a key scientific interest. Herein, solution-based dimension control strategies are proposed for copper chloride thiourea (CuCl-TU) coordination polymers that enable centimeter-scale, 2D nanosheet formation for use as transparent electrodes. Despite the wide bandgap of CuCl-TU polymers (4.33 eV), through polaron-mediated electron transfer, the electrical conductivity of the 2D sheet at room temperature is able to reach 4.45 S cm−1 without intentional doping. This leads to a highly anisotropic electronic conductivity of up to the order of ≈103 differences, depending on the material orientation. Furthermore, by substituting alternative thiourea candidates, it is demonstrated that it is possible to predesign CuCl-TU structures with the desired functionality, stability, and porosity through dimensional control. These findings provide a blueprint to design next-generation transparent conducting materials that can operate at room temperature, thereby expanding their applicability to different fields.


## Layman's Summary
**The Concept:** 
Imagine a material that is completely see-through like glass, but conducts electricity like a metal wire, and is as flexible as plastic. Scientists are trying to build these "transparent electrodes" by carefully stacking molecules at the [[Nanoscale|nanoscale]] into ultra-thin, flat sheets. This is the holy grail for creating next-generation screens, wearable electronics, and advanced smart devices.

**The Problem:** 
Making these flat, two-dimensional sheets in a liquid is notoriously difficult. Normally, as the sheets grow, they tend to clump up, get too thick, or roll themselves into tubes. When they get thick or clumped, they lose their transparency and their ability to conduct electricity well. Scientists usually face a stubborn trade-off: if you alter the material to make it a better electrical conductor, it almost always becomes less transparent, and vice versa. 

**The Solution:** 
The research team invented a clever way to grow and control sheets made of a special material called a copper chloride thiourea (CuCl-TU) coordination polymer (a highly structured type of [[Polymers|polymer]]). 
* Instead of simply stirring the liquid ingredients together, they carefully layered two different liquids and let the chemicals slowly mix at the boundary.
* This environment allows the material to [[Self-assembly|self-assemble]] into perfect, ultra-thin 2D sheets.
* Because the sheets naturally want to roll up to save space, the scientists quickly "trapped" them by pressing them onto a flat, water-loving plastic film before the rolling could happen.

**Why This is Special:** 
* **Massive but Microscopic:** The sheets they created are over a centimeter wide (which is huge for this kind of material) but remain incredibly thin—less than 5 nanometers thick!
* **Rule-Breaking Conductivity:** It conducts electricity incredibly well without sacrificing any of its optical transparency, completely bypassing the usual trade-off.
* **One-Way Street for Electrons:** It has massive "electronic anisotropy." This means electricity flows up to a million times easier in one specific flat direction than in the other.
* **Clever Chemistry:** Despite having a structural property (a wide bandgap) that normally prevents electrical flow, the material uses special charge carriers called "polarons" that allow electrons to effortlessly hop across the sheet.

**The Takeaway:** 
This breakthrough provides a brand-new recipe for creating highly efficient, transparent conductors. Because these materials can be manufactured easily in liquid solutions and operate perfectly at room temperature, they pave the way for a future of flexible displays, invisible [[Sensors|sensors]], and advanced electronic devices that are cheaper and easier to mass-produce.
