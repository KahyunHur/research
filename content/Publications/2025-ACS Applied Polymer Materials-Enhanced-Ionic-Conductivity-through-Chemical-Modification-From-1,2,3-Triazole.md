# Enhanced Ionic Conductivity through Chemical Modification: From 1,2,3-Triazole to 1,2,3-Triazolium Salt in Solid Polymer Electrolytes

**Authors:** Jo, Soyoung; Jung, Dayoung; Byun, Jaeduk; Yu, Seonmyeong; Hur, Kahyun; Cho, Byoung-Ki
**Journal:** ACS Applied Polymer Materials
**Year:** 2025
**Volume:** 7
**Pages:** 2184-2194
**Link:** [DOI: 10.1021/acsapm.4c02654](https://doi.org/10.1021/acsapm.4c02654)

## Abstract / Summary
Poly(ionic liquid)s (PILs) that combine the advantages of both polymers and ILs are promising solid polymer electrolytes (SPEs) for overcoming the drawbacks of liquid electrolytes. In this study, we report the synthesis and ionic conduction properties of two cross-linked polymer electrolytes (TR-P and TL-P) based on 1,2,3-triazole and 1,2,3-triazolium salt linkage groups, respectively. The unique approach in this study is the chemical modification from triazole to triazolium salt to increase ionic conduction. Through photopolymerization, both SPEs can be obtained as free-standing films. Notably, the electrochemical analysis demonstrates that the ionic conductivity of TL-P is 5.5 times greater than that of TR-P. This considerable increase in ionic conductivity arises from differences in the interaction between Li+ and the two linkage groups. For TR-P, the nitrogen atoms of the triazole group can tightly bind Li+, thus retarding ion conduction. In contrast, the modification into a triazolium salt in TL-P repels Li+, facilitating ion conduction. This experimental result is further supported by energy simulations, where attractive and repulsive interactions with Li+ are dominant in TR-P and TL-P, respectively. Consequently, simple chemical modification is strongly suggested as a way to improve the ionic conductivity of SPEs.


## Layman's Summary
**The Concept:**
Scientists are trying to make better and safer batteries by replacing the flammable liquids inside them with solid, flexible materials. These solid materials, known as [[Polymer Electrolytes|solid polymer electrolytes]], need to allow electricity-carrying particles to move through them quickly and freely in order for the battery to work well.

**The Problem:**
Most batteries today use a liquid to move ions around, but these liquids can leak and easily catch fire. While solid alternatives exist, they have a major flaw: the [[Polymers|polymers]] inside them often hold onto the moving particles—specifically [[Lithium Ions|lithium ions]]—too tightly. When the solid material acts like a sticky trap, the ions can't move fast enough, resulting in a battery that is sluggish and less efficient. 

**The Solution:**
The research team focused on a specific chemical building block (a "1,2,3-triazole" group) and made a tiny, targeted modification to it, converting it into a "triazolium salt." They then linked these modified molecules together using light to form a solid, free-standing film that can be used inside a battery. 

**Why This is Special:**
*   **Repelling Instead of Trapping:** The original chemical structure acted like a magnet, tightly binding the [[Lithium Ions|lithium ions]] and slowing them down. The new, modified structure actively repels the ions. 
*   **Faster Flow:** Because the modified material pushes the ions away rather than grabbing them, the ions are free to zip through the solid film quickly.
*   **Massive Performance Boost:** This simple chemical tweak increased the speed of the ions (ionic conductivity) by an impressive 5.5 times compared to the original material.
*   **Safe and Flexible:** The final product is a flexible, solid film that completely eliminates the safety risks associated with leaky, flammable liquid battery juices.

**The Takeaway:**
By making a smart, simple adjustment to the chemical makeup of a solid material, researchers were able to clear the "traffic jam" that normally slows down battery power. This discovery offers a clear and effective way to improve solid materials, bringing us one step closer to making future [[Lithium Batteries|lithium batteries]] that are both incredibly safe and highly efficient for electric vehicles and portable electronics.
