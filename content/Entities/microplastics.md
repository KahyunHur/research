# Microplastics

## What are Microplastics?
**Microplastics** are extremely small pieces of plastic debris in the environment resulting from the disposal and breakdown of consumer products and industrial waste. Officially, they are defined as any plastic fragment measuring less than 5 millimeters (0.2 inches) in length—about the size of a sesame seed or smaller. Many are so small they are completely invisible to the naked eye.

## Where Do They Come From?
Microplastics generally come from two main sources:
1. **Primary Microplastics:** These are plastics intentionally manufactured to be tiny. Examples include microbeads previously used in facial scrubs and cosmetics, or the raw plastic pellets (nurdles) used in factories to mold larger plastic items.
2. **Secondary Microplastics:** These are formed when larger plastic items—like water bottles, fishing nets, synthetic clothing, or car tires—break down over time. Exposure to sunlight (UV radiation), ocean waves, and physical wear-and-tear slowly degrades these large items into microscopic fragments.

## The Environmental and Health Problem
Because they are so small, microplastics easily pass through water filtration systems and end up in rivers, oceans, and soil. 

The major concerns surrounding microplastics include:
- **Food Chain Infiltration:** Marine life and insects often mistake these tiny particles for food. When smaller animals are eaten by larger ones, the plastics travel up the food chain, eventually ending up in the seafood and water consumed by humans.
- **Toxicity:** Plastics are not just empty material; they often contain harmful chemical additives used during manufacturing (like dyes and fire retardants). Furthermore, because of their surface properties, microplastics act like microscopic sponges, absorbing heavy metals and toxic pollutants from the surrounding environment.
- **Physical Damage:** Ingesting these particles can cause physical blockages or internal damage to the digestive systems of wildlife.

## Seeking Solutions
Microplastics represent a massive challenge in modern environmental and [[Materials Science]]. They highlight the urgent need for materials that are truly [[Degradability|degradable]]. Researchers are currently exploring bio-based alternatives—such as naturally derived [[Cellulose]] or advanced [[Biocompatible Materials]]—that can replace traditional, petroleum-based [[Polymers]] and safely break down into harmless organic matter without leaving a permanent, microscopic footprint on the planet.
