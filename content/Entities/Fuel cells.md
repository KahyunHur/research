# Fuel cells

Fuel cells are an important application in our research. 

Related to: [[Energy applications]]

## What is it?
Imagine a battery that never runs out of power as long as you keep feeding it fuel. That's essentially what a fuel cell does. While a regular battery stores a limited amount of chemical energy inside it, a fuel cell continuously generates [[electricity]] through a chemical reaction between a fuel (most commonly [[hydrogen]]) and an oxidant (like [[oxygen]] from the air). Just like a car engine needs gasoline and air to run, a fuel cell needs a steady supply of these gases to keep producing power. 

Inside the fuel cell, the [[hydrogen]] molecules are split into [[electrons]] and [[protons]]. The [[electrons]] are forced to travel through a circuit, which creates an electrical current that can power devices, before they recombine with the [[protons]] and [[oxygen]] to form a completely harmless byproduct: pure [[water]].

## Why is it Important?
Fuel cells are incredibly important because they offer a highly efficient and clean way to generate [[electricity]]. Because their only exhaust is [[water]] and heat, they are a zero-emission alternative to traditional combustion engines, making them a crucial technology in the fight against [[climate change]] and air pollution. They are highly versatile and can be scaled up or down, powering everything from small electronic devices to cars, buses, and even providing backup power for hospitals or entire buildings.

Furthermore, fuel cells are a key component of the transition towards a [[hydrogen economy]]. When paired with [[renewable energy]] sources—like using excess solar or wind power to produce [[hydrogen]] through [[electrolysis]]—fuel cells provide a reliable way to store and transport clean energy, releasing it exactly when and where it is needed without relying on fossil fuels.
